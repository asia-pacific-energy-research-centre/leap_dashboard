About this dashboard

This dashboard compares LEAP, ESTO_EXTENDED on one shared set of flow/product categories (the "common ESTO" axis). A source's native flow and product codes are mapped onto the common axis in leap_mappings, so a chart here is always comparing like with like even when the three sources describe the same fuel or sector differently.

Every page is generated automatically from that shared data — nothing on this page is hand-edited, and re-running the generator overwrites it completely. See codebase/common_esto_dashboard_workflow.py in the leap_dashboard repository to reproduce or update it.

Some LEAP economies still report demand through All demand aggregated while detailed branches are being developed. The dashboard uses the mapping-owned coverage record to decide which sector pages are transitional, uses declared TFC/TFEC totals for balance lines, and keeps aggregate-only emissions as one clearly labelled series rather than inventing a sector split. See the Emissions note and the source-selection CSV for the level used by each source.

Each rendered economy is a self-contained set of static files: this page, the other pages linked above, a chart_bundles/ folder holding each page's chart data, and a supporting_files/ folder holding the CSVs behind the charts. Pages load their charts from chart_bundles/ by a relative path, so copying a single .html file on its own will open the page but its charts will not draw — copy the whole economy folder (or the whole dashboards/ + chart_bundles/ pair) together whenever sharing a rendered dashboard.

Model guide

In the APERC LEAP system, the model is organised around the main LEAP branches of Demand, Transformation, and Resources. The dashboard sits outside LEAP and helps users check how these branches behave in the results, especially by comparing LEAP outputs with ESTO historical balances and 9th Outlook projections. The table below gives a short guide to the main model groups and how they should be understood when using either LEAP or the dashboard.

Main group What it covers in LEAP Main dashboard purpose What users should understand
Demand Final energy demand from buildings, industry, transport, agriculture, fishing, and other demand sectors. Shows how final energy use changes by sector, subsector, fuel, economy, and scenario. Demand is where most end-use assumptions sit. Some sectors are simple activity × intensity models, while buildings, industry, and road transport contain more detailed structures.
Buildings demand Residential and commercial/public services demand, usually split into end uses, fuels, activity, and intensity. Helps check fuel use, sector totals, and whether modelled building demand remains consistent with expected energy balance trends. Buildings is more detailed than a simple sector total. It is designed to preserve useful end-use and fuel-switching detail while still calibrating back to energy balance totals.
Industry demand Energy use by industrial subsector, with more detail for major energy-intensive activities and simpler treatment for smaller subsectors. Helps compare industrial fuel use, subsector demand, and large changes against historical and projected values. Industry is a hybrid model. Some parts are technology or process based, while others are simpler activity × intensity structures.
Road transport demand Passenger road and freight road transport. This includes vehicle stocks, sales, retirements, mileage, efficiency, vehicle types, engine types, and fuel use. Helps check road energy demand, vehicle stock, sales, turnover, fuel switching, and passenger/freight road trends. Road transport is the most detailed demand model. It uses stock-flow logic, so changes in sales, retirements, mileage, and efficiency all affect future energy demand.
Non-road and international transport demand Domestic aviation, domestic navigation/shipping, rail, pipelines, non-specified transport, and international aviation and marine bunkers where represented. Helps check non-road fuel use, bunker demand, pipeline demand, and domestic aviation/shipping/rail trends. Non-road transport is generally simpler than road transport. It is usually closer to an activity × intensity model, with calibration to ESTO energy totals where needed. International bunkers and pipelines are included here because they are transport-related flows but are not part of the road stock-flow model.
Other demand Agriculture, fishing, non-specified demand, and other relatively small final-demand sectors. Helps check whether smaller demand sectors remain stable, plausible, and aligned with the balance structure. These sectors are usually simpler and are mainly included to preserve the full energy balance structure.
Transformation Energy conversion sectors such as electricity generation, heat generation, refining, transfers, LNG regasification, NG liquefaction, gas processing, coal transformation, coke ovens, blast furnaces, and other transformation processes. Shows transformation inputs, outputs, losses, efficiencies, and whether conversion sectors are producing plausible results. Transformation is where fuels are converted into other fuels. Some modules are physical processes, while others mainly exist to reproduce the structure of the energy balance.
Power and heat Electricity and heat generation, including generation technologies, capacity, dispatch, load shapes, losses, and optimisation settings. Helps check electricity and heat output, generation mix, capacity, fuel inputs, losses, and unusual dispatch outcomes. Power is the most complex transformation area because it may use NEMO optimisation inside LEAP. It usually needs more manual checking than simpler transformation sectors.
Refining Oil refining inputs, outputs, product yields, capacity assumptions, and refinery-related fuel production. Helps check refinery feedstocks, oil product outputs, product balances, and whether refining capacity or output shares are creating plausible results. Refining is kept separate because it is a major transformation process with a clear physical interpretation: one or more inputs are converted into multiple oil-product outputs. It needs careful checking because product output shares can create surplus or shortage issues.
Other transformation, including transfers LNG regasification, NG liquefaction, gas processing/blending, coal transformation, coke ovens, blast furnaces, patent fuel plants, non-specified transformation, upstream liquids transfers, refinery/blending transfers, and transfers unallocated. Helps identify unusual transformation outputs, transfer flows, capacity limits, shortfalls, surpluses, and fuel-balance problems. This group contains a mixture of physical processes and balance-structure modules. Transfers are included here because they mainly organise ESTO transfer flows and may contain simplified or non-physical input-output relationships used to preserve balance-table consistency.
Resources / supply Domestic production, imports, exports, primary resources, and secondary fuel trade. Shows whether fuels are being supplied through the intended mix of domestic production, imports, exports, and transformation outputs. Resources is where LEAP balances remaining fuel requirements. Imports and exports are useful diagnostics because they reveal whether the rest of the system is producing too much or too little.
Reconciliation / QA The whole-system checking process after LEAP has calculated demand, transformation, resources, imports, exports, and losses. Highlights differences between LEAP results, ESTO historical balances, and 9th Outlook projections. Reconciliation is not a separate LEAP branch. It is the checking layer that shows whether the integrated model is still aligned with the intended energy balance.
Dashboard External results and checking interface built from LEAP exports, ESTO data, and comparison datasets. Makes it easier to inspect sector totals, fuel totals, differences, percentage differences, and suspicious changes across the model. The dashboard is not the model itself. It is the main tool for reviewing whether the LEAP model behaves sensibly and whether further adjustment is needed.
